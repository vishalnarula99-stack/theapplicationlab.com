From a research idea to a proposal blueprint

01The problem

You may know what you want to research. You may have already started reading around it. But turning that idea into a PhD proposal raises a different set of questions.

  • What exactly is the research problem?
  • Is there a convincing gap?
  • Are your research questions actually answerable?
  • What is your contribution to the larger discipline?
  • Do you need a theoretical framework?
  • Which methods make sense for your research question(s)?
  • And how do you bring all of that together into a proposal that a potential supervisor can follow and support?

Across the two days, early-career researchers and PhD candidates from Oxford, Cambridge, LSE, Warwick and Nottingham will take you through the different stages of developing a proposal, from refining the research problem and questions to literature, theory, methodology, proposal writing, supervisor fit and funding.

But this is not just a series of talks.

You will work on your own research ideas throughout the workshop, using the TAL PhD Proposal Blueprint. There will be two dedicated Proposal Labs where you will work in smaller breakout rooms with our facilitators and mentors and receive feedback on how your project is developing.

By the end of the workshop, the aim is for you to leave with a much clearer blueprint for your proposal: your research problem and provisional questions, literature and potential gap, contribution, conceptual direction, research design and methods, proposal structure, and your next steps towards approaching supervisors and developing a full draft.

02Fit criteria

Who is this workshop for?

This workshop is for prospective PhD applicants who already have a research idea they want to develop into a proposal.

You do not need to arrive with a finished proposal or a perfectly formulated research question. But you should be able to explain, in a few sentences, what you want to study, why you are interested in it, and the kind of question or problem you may want to investigate. You should also have done some preliminary reading around your topic.

This workshop is not designed for those who are still at the very beginning of deciding what they might want to pursue for a PhD. If your starting point is still quite broad, for example, "I want to do something on gender," "I'm interested in AI," or "I want to do a PhD, but I'm not yet sure what I want to research", we recommend spending some more time developing and narrowing your research interests before joining the workshop.

03Workshop aim & outcomes

Workshop aim

A good PhD proposal is not simply a collection of separate sections on literature, theory, methodology, and outcomes. It needs to make a coherent pitch for a research project: What is the problem/gap in our understanding of that particular problem? What do we not yet understand? What question will you answer? Why does it matter? What evidence/data will you need? And can the project actually be done/is it feasible?

Over the two days, we will work through these questions in sequence. Alongside taught sessions, participants will work on their own projects in breakout rooms with the workshop facilitators and mentors.

Participants will use a structured TAL PhD proposal blueprint throughout the workshop, so that the work done in one session feeds into the next.

By the end of the workshop, participants should have:

  • A clearly articulated research problem and provisional research question(s)
  • A map of the key literature and a potential research gap
  • A preliminary statement of the project's contribution and significance
  • A theoretical or conceptual direction, where appropriate
  • An appropriate research design and proposed methods
  • A section-by-section structure for the research proposal
  • A strategy for identifying and approaching potential supervisors and target universities
  • A personalised proposal blueprint from which to begin or revise the full proposal

What is the literature review actually doing in a PhD proposal?

We will then look at how to identify a defensible research gap, move beyond simply saying "this has not been studied before", and explain what your project will contribute and why that contribution matters.

Speaker: Feryal Banday, PhD Candidate in English, University of Cambridge

Speaker: Feryal Banday, PhD Candidate in English, University of Cambridge

What kinds of research questions can be answered through archives, documents and texts?

This session will introduce different ways of designing archival and textual research, including what can count as an archive, document or textual source, how sources are identified and selected, and different approaches to their analysis. It will focus on the key choices applicants need to make: identifying appropriate sources, defining the scope of the material, navigating access and feasibility, recognising the limitations and silences of archives, and addressing ethical considerations. It will also consider how to explain and justify archival and textual methods within a PhD proposal.

5. Designing a Qualitative Research Project: Approaches, Methods and Analysis
Qualitative approaches and methods, from interviews and focus groups to ethnography and case studies; selecting participants or cases; navigating access and ethics; analysing qualitative data; and justifying methodological choices.
Speaker: Preeti Pratishruti Dash, PhD Candidate in Law, University of Cambridge

Speaker: Preeti Pratishruti Dash, PhD Candidate in Law, University of Cambridge

How do you decide which qualitative methodology and methods will best answer your research question?

This session introduces different ways of designing qualitative research, distinguishing between methodological approaches, methods of data collection and methods of analysis. We will explore approaches such as ethnography, case study, phenomenology and grounded theory, alongside methods including interviews, focus groups, observation, life histories, and visual and participatory methods.

The session will focus on the key choices applicants need to make: selecting participants or cases, choosing methods that fit their research questions, navigating access and ethics, and planning analysis. We will also briefly introduce comparative and mixed-methods designs, with a particular focus on how to justify methodological choices rather than simply listing methods in a proposal.
